What to Do When You Find Aphids on Plants

By Cozy Green Nook Editorial Team · Published October 5, 2026 · 4 min read

Aphids gather on tender growth and can curl leaves or leave sticky honeydew. Confirm the insect and assess how much of the plant is affected before intervening.

Quick Take

Isolate indoor plants where practical, rinse small infestations carefully, and use labeled controls only when needed.

Checking aphid activity after treatment

Aphids cluster on tender stems and new leaves, so inspect those areas again after a control step. Count or photograph the affected growth rather than reacting to a single insect; beneficial insects may already be reducing the colony. Distorted older leaves may not recover even when aphids are gone. If numbers keep rising, confirm the identification and follow the product label before repeating treatment.

A useful follow-up is to inspect the growing tip and the undersides of nearby leaves at the same time of day. Record whether live aphids are present, not just whether old leaves remain curled. Lady beetles, lacewing larvae, and other predators may be feeding; avoid broad treatments that harm them when a small colony is already declining. Recheck the label before any repeat application.

Interpreting leaf damage after aphid control

Check whether the newest leaves remain sticky and whether the growing tip is still distorted. Honeydew can attract ants or support sooty mold, while old curled leaves may persist after the insects are gone. If live aphids have disappeared, allow new growth to show whether the plant is recovering before taking another control step.

After rinsing aphids away, inspect the growing tip and the undersides of nearby leaves a day or two later. New clusters on soft growth suggest that more monitoring is needed; a few isolated insects may be manageable without spraying. Keep beneficial insects in place and avoid treating flowers that pollinators visit. Follow any pesticide label for timing and repeat use.

Checking aphid damage over time

Aphids often cluster on tender growth, but curled leaves may remain distorted after the insects are gone. Check for live insects, fresh honeydew, and new growth before repeating a treatment. Rinse sturdy plants gently if appropriate, then let foliage dry in a suitable location. Avoid spraying open flowers where pollinators may visit. If aphids return, confirm that the product you use is labeled for the plant and setting. Take a photo before and after treatment so you can judge whether the infestation is declining.

Check whether aphids are active before choosing a response

Aphids often cluster on tender stems and new leaves, where they may leave sticky honeydew or cause curled growth. Inspect the undersides of nearby leaves and look for live insects; ants or curled leaves alone do not confirm aphids. Lady beetles, lacewings, and other beneficial insects may be present, so identify what you see before treating. Photograph the affected shoot and note whether fresh colonies or damage appear over the next few days.

For a small, confirmed colony, remove the insects by hand or prune a heavily affected tip when that will not harm the plant. A gentle water rinse may work on sturdy plants, but it can damage delicate foliage or spread pests if done carelessly. Reinspect new growth and nearby plants. If using a product, verify the label covers aphids and the specific plant; edible crops require special attention to harvest intervals. Do not use dish soap or oil mixtures as a substitute for a labeled product. Judge success by whether live aphids and new damage decline, not by whether old curled leaves regain their shape.

Know when aphid damage needs a stronger response

A few aphids on a vigorous plant may be controlled by natural predators or simple removal, while a growing colony on tender shoots can distort new leaves and weaken seedlings. Check the number of live insects and whether fresh damage is appearing; old curled leaves may remain distorted after the aphids are gone. Look for lady beetles, lacewing larvae, or other beneficial insects before treating. Avoid spraying when pollinators are visiting flowers.

Start with a method suited to the plant: hand removal or pruning a limited infested tip may be enough. A gentle rinse can dislodge aphids from sturdy foliage, but protect soil and avoid spreading pests to nearby plants. Reinspect new growth after a few days. If a product is needed, use only one labeled for aphids and the plant or crop, and follow its full instructions. For vegetables, observe the stated harvest interval.

Do not apply a homemade dish-soap mix; household detergents can burn leaves and are not standardized pesticides. If repeated colonies return, check nearby host plants and growing conditions, and seek identification from an extension service. Record dates, affected shoots, and action taken. That history helps show whether the population is declining and prevents unnecessary repeat treatment.

Separate aphids from other causes of curled new leaves

Curled leaves may be caused by aphids, but heat, herbicide drift, virus, or watering stress can create similar symptoms. Inspect the underside and folds of new leaves for live aphids, shed skins, and honeydew. Look for ants tending the colony and beneficial insects nearby. Photograph both affected and unaffected growth before treatment. If no aphids are visible, ask an extension service to identify the issue rather than spraying based on leaf shape alone.

Aphids can reproduce quickly in favorable conditions, so revisit the plant after a few days. Remove a small colony manually or prune a severely infested tip when the plant can tolerate it. A water rinse may help robust plants, but avoid spreading insects to neighboring crops. For edible plants, use only products labeled for the crop and follow the preharvest interval exactly.

Old curled leaves may not uncurl after aphids are gone; assess new leaves for normal shape and check for live insects. Record the date and any actions taken. If colonies return, inspect nearby weeds and host plants and preserve beneficial insects where possible. Avoid household soap and oil mixtures, which can burn foliage and are not a substitute for a labeled control.

In Summary

Isolate indoor plants where practical, rinse small infestations carefully, and use labeled controls only when needed. Confirm live aphids before acting, protect beneficial insects, and judge results by new growth.
