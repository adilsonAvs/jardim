What to Do When You Find Aphids on Plants

By Cozy Green Nook Editorial Team · Published October 5, 2026 · 4 min read

Aphids gather on tender growth and can curl leaves or leave sticky honeydew. Confirm the insect and assess how much of the plant is affected before intervening.

Quick Take

Isolate indoor plants where practical, rinse small infestations carefully, and use labeled controls only when needed.

Choosing a proportionate response to aphids

A small colony on a sturdy plant may be manageable by rinsing or removing the affected tip, while a tender seedling may need a different response. Protect the potting mix from flooding and avoid knocking beneficial insects onto the ground. Sticky honeydew can attract ants and support sooty mold, so clean nearby surfaces after addressing the colony. Check the plant again and look for new aphids on fresh growth. If you use a pesticide, confirm that aphids and the plant are listed on the label and follow all precautions. Repeated applications should follow label timing, not an improvised schedule.

Choosing a proportionate response to aphids

A small aphid colony on a sturdy plant may be manageable by rinsing or removing an affected tip, while a tender seedling may need a different response. Look for new colonies on tender growth and leaf undersides after taking action. Sticky honeydew can attract ants and support sooty mold, so clean nearby surfaces after addressing the insects. Avoid knocking beneficial insects off the plant or spraying them. If using a pesticide, confirm the label includes aphids, the plant, and the setting. Repeat applications must follow label timing rather than an improvised schedule. Seek identification if the damage continues despite a reasonable response.

Checking aphid damage over time

Aphids often cluster on tender growth, but curled leaves may remain distorted after the insects are gone. Check for live insects, fresh honeydew, and new growth before repeating a treatment. Rinse sturdy plants gently if appropriate, then let foliage dry in a suitable location. Avoid spraying open flowers where pollinators may visit. If aphids return, confirm that the product you use is labeled for the plant and setting. Take a photo before and after treatment so you can judge whether the infestation is declining.

Inspect leaf undersides and stems

Check how much light the spot gets throughout the day, whether it receives direct sun, drafts, reflected heat, or cold spells. Also check that water drains through the pot and does not sit in a saucer. For dealing with aphids on plants, growing conditions matter as much as technique. The same routine may work in one setting and be too much in another.

Inspect for confirming signs

For dealing with aphids on plants, record when the signs began, which parts of the plant are affected, and whether the problem is spreading. Inspect both sides of leaves, stems, new growth, and the potting mix. Take close-up photos and one of the whole plant to show the pattern. Look for insects, webbing, spots, distortions, or sticky residue, but do not settle on a cause before identifying it. Watering, sun, and temperature damage can resemble pest symptoms.

Identify the cause

If you see an organism, identify it before choosing a control method. Some species are beneficial and do not need to be removed; others call for prompt management. Isolate the affected plant when doing so can limit spread without causing additional stress. Clean tools and wash your hands after handling it. Bag infested leaves before carrying them through the home. Keep children and pets away from suspect plants and any products used to treat them.

Choose a measured response

Start with the lowest-risk measures that fit the confirmed problem, such as removing a small number of insects by hand or discarding badly affected parts. Do not spray with force if that could spread the problem or damage the plant. Homemade remedies are not automatically safe: the wrong soap, alcohol, vinegar, and oils can burn leaves or contaminate food. Use only products labeled for the intended purpose, follow the label, and keep them away from people and pets.

Monitor for recurrence

After taking action, check the plants at reasonable intervals and reassess the identification if symptoms change. Do not assume one application is a universal fix; follow the product’s specific directions and never increase application frequency on your own. Dispose of residue according to local guidance. If the problem spreads quickly or affects several plants, contact a local extension service, horticulturist, or trusted nursery with photos and notes about the growing conditions.

Keep in Mind

In Summary

Isolate indoor plants where practical, rinse small infestations carefully, and use labeled controls only when needed. Record what you observe so your next decision is based on the plant's response.
