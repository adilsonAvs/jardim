How to Choose Pet-Safe Plants for Homes with Cats and Dogs

By Cozy Green Nook Editorial Team · Published October 5, 2026 · 4 min read

Plant safety depends on the exact species and the amount eaten. Verify botanical names with a veterinary poison resource before bringing a plant into a home with pets.

Quick Take

Keep uncertain plants out of reach and choose verified non-toxic options; contact a veterinarian if a pet chews a plant.

Verifying a plant before bringing it home

Common names can refer to several species. Check the botanical name on the plant tag and compare it with a veterinary poison-control or reputable animal-health resource before placing it within reach. “Pet-safe” does not mean a plant should be eaten; chewing can still cause stomach upset or choking. If the identity is uncertain, choose a verified alternative or keep the plant inaccessible. Save the tag and a photo in case you need to identify it later. If a pet chews a plant, contact a veterinarian for advice and share the plant name or photo instead of waiting for symptoms or trying a home remedy.

What to do after a pet chews a plant

If a pet has chewed a plant, take a clear photo of the whole plant and a close-up of the leaves, and keep the nursery label if available. Contact a veterinarian or animal poison-control service for guidance, especially if the species is uncertain or the pet shows symptoms. Do not induce vomiting or give a home remedy unless a veterinary professional directs you to do so. Keep the remaining plant and fallen leaves out of reach. Accurate identification helps a professional determine the right next step.

Make a plant-by-plant safety check for pets

Before bringing a plant home, record both its common and botanical name from the nursery tag and verify that exact species with a veterinary poison-control or reputable animal-health source. Common names can refer to unrelated plants, and cultivars may differ. Check whether the plant contains irritating sap and whether fallen leaves, seeds, or cuttings could be reached by a curious animal. A plant described as non-toxic still is not food; chewing can cause choking or stomach upset.

Plan placement around the pet’s habits rather than relying only on shelf height. Cats may reach trailing vines, while dogs may knock over a floor pot. Remove fallen leaves promptly and keep pruning waste inaccessible. If identity cannot be confirmed, choose a verified alternative and keep the uncertain plant separated. If a pet chews a plant, take a photo and contact a veterinarian or animal poison-control service; do not induce vomiting or use a home remedy unless directed by a professional. Keep a record of plant names and sources so the household can respond quickly if exposure occurs.

Prepare a household plant inventory for veterinary emergencies

Keep a list of every plant in the home with its common name, botanical name if known, where it is kept, and a clear photograph. Save the nursery label or link to the toxicity source used to verify it. Common names can be ambiguous, so an accurate identity matters if a pet chews a leaf. Update the list when you bring home a new plant, propagate one, or move cuttings to another room.

Make access difficult for pets that chew foliage, dig in pots, or knock containers over. A hanging basket is not automatically out of reach for a cat, and a stable floor pot may still be accessible to a dog. Remove fallen leaves and keep pruning debris in a closed waste container. Avoid using fertilizers or pesticides where a pet can reach the product or treated plant; follow label directions and keep pets away for the required interval.

If chewing occurs, note the time, approximate amount, and plant identity, then contact a veterinarian or animal poison-control service promptly. Take a photo or bring the label if instructed. Do not wait for symptoms or try to induce vomiting unless a veterinary professional tells you to. This practical record cannot replace veterinary advice, but it can help a professional assess the exposure accurately.

Review plant safety whenever the household changes

New pets, puppies that begin exploring, moved furniture, or a growing vine can change which plants are reachable. Recheck placement when an animal’s behavior or the room layout changes. Keep cuttings, fallen leaves, seeds, and plant labels organized so you can identify material if a pet chews it. Do not assume a plant is safe because an animal ignored it previously; exposure and chewing habits vary.

Verify each new plant by botanical name before bringing it home. Use a veterinary poison resource or animal-health source, not only a seller’s common-name label. If a source is unclear, choose a different plant or keep it out of reach until confirmed. Even plants classified as non-toxic can cause irritation or stomach upset when eaten, and large pieces can be a choking hazard.

Update the household inventory with a photo and source for each plant. If exposure occurs, contact a veterinarian or animal poison-control service with the plant name, amount, and time. Do not wait for symptoms or give home remedies without professional direction. This routine helps the household make safer choices while recognizing that plant toxicity depends on the exact species and exposure.

In Summary

Keep uncertain plants out of reach and choose verified non-toxic options; contact a veterinarian if a pet chews a plant. Verify each plant by botanical name and contact a veterinarian promptly if a pet chews it.
