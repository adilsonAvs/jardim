How to Identify Spider Mites on Houseplants

By Adilson V. Santos · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026 · 6 min read

Spider mites are tiny and easy to miss. Look for fine stippling, pale leaves, and delicate webbing, then confirm the pest before choosing a treatment.

Quick Take

Inspect leaf undersides with a light and isolate a confirmed infested plant while you plan a labeled, plant-safe response.

What webbing can confirm

Webbing can support a spider-mite diagnosis, especially when it appears with fine stippling, but not every web on a houseplant comes from mites. Check whether new pale marks appear on leaves after isolation and inspect several leaf undersides with a hand lens. If you cannot see the pest, save clear photos and ask an extension service or nursery before applying a treatment.

Separating mite damage from other stress

Leaf stippling can also follow environmental stress, so compare affected and unaffected parts of the same plant. Spider mites are tiny; webbing may appear only when populations are larger. Keep a photo of the leaf undersides and check whether fresh damage appears after isolation. A predatory mite or other beneficial organism should not be treated as a pest. If you cannot identify what you see, send close photographs to a local extension service before applying a product.

What to document before and after mite management

Use a repeatable inspection instead of relying on memory. Select three leaves at different heights, including one with fresh stippling, and photograph their upper and lower surfaces in similar light. Note whether the marks are new, whether fine webbing is present, and whether moving specks can be seen with a hand lens. Record nearby plants and room conditions such as a hot window or dry air. These details help distinguish a developing infestation from old damage or another source of stress.

After isolating a plant, keep it separated while you confirm the pest and decide on a response. Inspect on a consistent schedule and compare the same leaves, but do not assume a single inspection proves that all mites are gone. Check the plant label and any treatment label for the specific pest and indoor use. A product that controls insects may not control mites. Never combine treatments or exceed application directions. If symptoms worsen despite correct use, stop and reassess rather than spraying more often.

Spider mites can spread on plant material, tools, or hands, so clean the work surface and tools after handling a confirmed case. Avoid carrying loose leaves past other plants. If you cannot identify the organism, share sharp close-ups and a whole-plant photo with a local extension service. This record helps an expert see both the suspected pest and the pattern of damage. The aim is a measured response tied to confirmed evidence, not routine preventive pesticide use.

Decide when mite damage warrants expert identification

Do not use webbing alone to conclude that spider mites are present. Ordinary spiders may leave webs without causing plant damage, and stippling can follow other stress. Use a hand lens to look for tiny moving mites near the damaged area and compare several leaves. If you cannot see the organism, photograph the underside at high resolution and ask a local extension office or qualified nursery to identify it before choosing a miticide.

Consider the plant’s value and the extent of damage when choosing next steps. A small affected plant can often be isolated and monitored; a severe infestation across a collection may need a more deliberate plan. Check the label for the exact mite and indoor plant use, along with ventilation, protective equipment, and re-entry instructions. Some products harm beneficial predatory mites, so avoid broad treatment when identification is uncertain.

After management, keep a dated checklist of fresh stippling, live mites, webbing, and new leaf growth. Do not interpret old damaged leaves as evidence that treatment failed; they may remain marked. If new damage continues, review identification and label directions rather than increasing the dose or frequency. A qualified local source can help reassess the diagnosis using your images and notes.

A practical spider-mite inspection log

Choose one affected leaf and one apparently healthy leaf on the same plant. Photograph both from the same distance, including the underside, and note the date, room, window exposure, and recent watering. A phone image may show stippling or webbing but may not resolve the mites themselves, so use a hand lens or ask a local extension office for identification when the organism is unclear. Check neighboring plants separately instead of assuming that every pale mark has the same cause.

After confirming spider mites, isolate the plant if practical and inspect it again on a consistent schedule. Record whether fresh stippling appears, whether moving specks are visible, and whether the pattern is spreading to new leaves. Use the label to confirm any product is intended for spider mites and indoor ornamentals; follow its directions exactly. If the evidence points to another pest or environmental stress, change the diagnosis before choosing a treatment. This record makes follow-up specific to the plant and its room rather than a blanket spray routine.

A closer inspection routine

Use a white sheet of paper as a simple inspection aid: hold it beneath a leaf and tap the leaf gently, then look for tiny moving specks. This does not replace a hand lens or identification, but it can help distinguish active pests from old spotting. Spider mite damage often develops as many small pale marks rather than one isolated lesion. Check several leaves and compare the pattern on plants nearby. If you choose a miticide or insecticidal soap, verify that the label includes spider mites and indoor houseplants; products differ in what they control. Follow re-entry, ventilation, and repeat-application directions exactly, and do not mix treatments.

In Summary

Inspect leaf undersides with a light and isolate a confirmed infested plant while you plan a labeled, plant-safe response. Compare fresh leaf damage and confirmed moving mites before choosing a labeled treatment; inspect again on the label’s schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if a houseplant has spider mites?

Look for fine pale stippling, especially on leaf undersides, and check for tiny moving mites or delicate webbing. A hand lens and clear photos help distinguish active pests from old damage.

Should I isolate a plant with suspected spider mites?

Separate it while you inspect nearby plants. Confirm the pest before treating; some webbing and leaf marks have other causes.

Can I use any insect spray for spider mites?

No. Use only a product whose label includes spider mites and indoor houseplants, and follow its ventilation, re-entry, and repeat-use directions.
