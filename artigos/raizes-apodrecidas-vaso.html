How to Spot Root Rot in Potted Plants

By Cozy Green Nook Editorial Team · Published October 5, 2026 · 4 min read

Root rot is associated with persistently wet conditions, but yellow leaves alone do not confirm it. Check drainage and root condition before repotting or cutting roots.

Quick Take

Look for soft, dark roots and sour-smelling mix; remove only clearly damaged roots with clean tools and correct the drainage cause.

When to stop and seek help

If the plant has few healthy roots left, its recovery can be uncertain and will depend on the species and growing conditions. Avoid repeated unpotting, which can damage remaining roots. Use a clean container with open drainage and a medium appropriate to the plant. Do not place fertilizer, fungicide, or a home remedy directly on injured roots unless a reliable source and product label support that use. Keep a photo of the roots and note how long the mix stayed wet. A local extension service or experienced nursery may help distinguish root rot from normal root color or other causes.

Why drainage correction matters

Removing damaged roots will not solve the problem if the container continues to hold excess water. Check whether drainage holes are blocked, whether a decorative pot traps runoff, and whether the growing medium has compacted. Use a fresh, appropriate mix only when the old one is failing or contaminated; repotting itself can add stress. Do not place gravel at the bottom as a substitute for drainage holes, because water must still be able to leave the pot. After repotting, keep conditions steady and let the plant use the remaining healthy roots. Follow species-specific watering guidance as recovery begins.

Caring for roots after repotting

After removing damaged roots, use a clean pot with open drainage and fresh medium suited to the plant. Water to settle the mix, then let the species’ normal drying pattern guide the next watering. Do not add fertilizer while roots are injured unless reliable species guidance recommends it. Avoid repeated unpotting; assess recovery through firm stems and healthy new growth over time.

Check for root rot before disturbing the root ball

Wilting in wet mix, yellowing, soft stems, and a sour smell can raise concern about root rot, but each symptom has other possible causes. Check whether the pot drains freely and whether water remains in a cachepot. If the plant continues to decline despite correcting standing water, slide the root ball out gently and inspect a small area. Healthy roots are usually firm; dark, mushy roots that smell unpleasant are more concerning. Do not diagnose from root color alone because some species naturally have darker roots.

If rot is confirmed, use clean scissors to remove only soft, damaged tissue and repot in a clean container with open drainage and fresh mix suitable for the species. Do not use a pot much larger than the remaining root system. Water according to the plant’s needs and allow excess to drain. Clean tools and discard infected material rather than reusing it. Take photos before and after, and record how quickly the new mix dries. If most roots are affected or the plant is valuable, ask a local extension service or experienced horticulturist for help before applying fungicides.

Choose aftercare based on how much root tissue remains

After removing clearly rotten roots, compare the remaining root mass with the pot size. A smaller root system usually cannot use water from an oversized container quickly, so select a clean pot that fits the remaining roots and drains freely. Use fresh mix appropriate for the species and avoid packing it tightly. Do not add fertilizer to compensate for lost roots; damaged roots may be sensitive to salts. Keep the plant in stable light and temperature while it recovers.

Water only as the species and mix require, then empty the saucer. A plant with fewer roots may use less water, but it should not be left bone dry if it normally needs moisture. Check below the surface and lift the pot to learn its new drying pattern. Avoid repeated unpotting to inspect progress, since that can damage new roots. Record a photo, date, pot size, and watering so changes can be compared over time.

Recovery is gradual. Old leaves may remain yellow or drop, while healthy new growth is a better sign that roots are functioning. If the stem base continues to soften, the smell persists, or the plant declines despite corrected drainage, seek help from an extension service or horticulturist. Do not apply fungicide without a specific diagnosis and a label that covers the plant and problem.

Recognize when a root problem needs local help

Repotting is not always the answer to every wilting plant. If only one root is damaged and most roots remain firm, correcting drainage and watering may be enough. If the root mass is largely soft, dark, or foul-smelling, the plant may have little healthy tissue left. Species, season, and the value of the plant affect whether recovery is practical. Photograph the roots and the whole plant before trimming so an expert can see the pattern.

Ask an extension service, botanical garden, or experienced horticulturist to review symptoms when the diagnosis is uncertain. Share the species name, pot type, mix, watering history, and how long symptoms have been present. Do not apply fungicide or household disinfectants to roots without a diagnosis and a label that specifically allows the use. Such products can damage remaining healthy tissue.

After any correction, keep the plant in stable conditions and avoid repeated root inspections. Record whether the mix dries normally and whether new growth appears. If recovery does not occur, it may be more practical to propagate a healthy cutting, if the species and plant condition allow it, than to continue stressing the remaining roots. Clean tools and discard infected material safely.

In Summary

Look for soft, dark roots and sour-smelling mix; remove only clearly damaged roots with clean tools and correct the drainage cause. Confirm root damage, improve drainage, and remove only rotten tissue before repotting a plant.
