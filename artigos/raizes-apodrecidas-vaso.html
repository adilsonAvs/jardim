How to Spot Root Rot in Potted Plants

By Cozy Green Nook Editorial Team · Published October 5, 2026 · 4 min read

Root rot is associated with persistently wet conditions, but yellow leaves alone do not confirm it. Check drainage and root condition before repotting or cutting roots.

Quick Take

Look for soft, dark roots and sour-smelling mix; remove only clearly damaged roots with clean tools and correct the drainage cause.

When to stop and seek help

If the plant has few healthy roots left, its recovery can be uncertain and will depend on the species and growing conditions. Avoid repeated unpotting, which can damage remaining roots. Use a clean container with open drainage and a medium appropriate to the plant. Do not place fertilizer, fungicide, or a home remedy directly on injured roots unless a reliable source and product label support that use. Keep a photo of the roots and note how long the mix stayed wet. A local extension service or experienced nursery may help distinguish root rot from normal root color or other causes.

Why drainage correction matters

Removing damaged roots will not solve the problem if the container continues to hold excess water. Check whether drainage holes are blocked, whether a decorative pot traps runoff, and whether the growing medium has compacted. Use a fresh, appropriate mix only when the old one is failing or contaminated; repotting itself can add stress. Do not place gravel at the bottom as a substitute for drainage holes, because water must still be able to leave the pot. After repotting, keep conditions steady and let the plant use the remaining healthy roots. Follow species-specific watering guidance as recovery begins.

Correcting the conditions around damaged roots

Removing damaged roots does not solve the problem if the pot still holds excess water. Check for blocked drainage holes, a decorative cover that traps runoff, or mix that has compacted. Do not unpot a plant for one yellow leaf; inspect roots when persistent wetness or decline supports that step. Use a fresh, appropriate medium only when the old one is failing, and choose a container with open drainage. Avoid gravel at the bottom as a substitute for a drainage hole. After repotting, keep conditions stable and follow the plant’s care needs while new roots recover.

Read leaves with the potting mix

Check how much light the spot gets throughout the day, whether it receives direct sun, drafts, reflected heat, or cold spells. Also check that water drains through the pot and does not sit in a saucer. For spotting signs of root rot in potted plants, growing conditions matter as much as technique. The same routine may work in one setting and be too much in another.

Investigate persistent symptoms

Check the root-zone moisture

For spotting signs of root rot in potted plants, record when the signs began, which parts of the plant are affected, and whether the problem is spreading. Inspect both sides of leaves, stems, new growth, and the potting mix. Take close-up photos and one of the whole plant to show the pattern. Look for insects, webbing, spots, distortions, or sticky residue, but do not settle on a cause before identifying it. Watering, sun, and temperature damage can resemble pest symptoms.

Read symptoms in context

If you see an organism, identify it before choosing a control method. Some species are beneficial and do not need to be removed; others call for prompt management. Isolate the affected plant when doing so can limit spread without causing additional stress. Clean tools and wash your hands after handling it. Bag infested leaves before carrying them through the home. Keep children and pets away from suspect plants and any products used to treat them.

Correct the watering method

Start with the lowest-risk measures that fit the confirmed problem, such as removing a small number of insects by hand or discarding badly affected parts. Do not spray with force if that could spread the problem or damage the plant. Homemade remedies are not automatically safe: the wrong soap, alcohol, vinegar, and oils can burn leaves or contaminate food. Use only products labeled for the intended purpose, follow the label, and keep them away from people and pets.

Protect roots and drainage

Track recovery

After taking action, check the plants at reasonable intervals and reassess the identification if symptoms change. Do not assume one application is a universal fix; follow the product’s specific directions and never increase application frequency on your own. Dispose of residue according to local guidance. If the problem spreads quickly or affects several plants, contact a local extension service, horticulturist, or trusted nursery with photos and notes about the growing conditions.

Keep in Mind

In Summary

Look for soft, dark roots and sour-smelling mix; remove only clearly damaged roots with clean tools and correct the drainage cause. Record what you observe so your next decision is based on the plant's response.
