How to Create a Small Herb Garden in Your Kitchen

By Adilson V. Santos · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated November 22, 2026 · 8 min read

A useful kitchen herb garden is organized around light, daily access, and how each plant uses water. Choose a small group that fits the brightest safe location, then arrange pots so cooking, cleaning, and harvesting remain easy.

Quick Take

Measure the usable window or counter, confirm at least six hours of direct light, as advised in Illinois Extension’s indoor-herb guidance, or plan supplemental lighting, and group plants by similar moisture needs rather than by appearance alone.

Start with the kitchen routine, not the number of pots

Decide how you will use the harvest before buying plants. A cook who wants a few fresh leaves for weekday meals needs a small, reachable setup; a person who wants enough to dry or preserve needs more light, growing area, and time. The University of Illinois Extension recommends considering the amount of work you want to do, your daily routine, and time away from home before planning an indoor kitchen garden.

Count the space that remains after everyday tasks. Measure the windowsill, counter, or shelf and mark the area needed for the faucet, cutting board, and food preparation. A row of pots can block a sink or collect splashes from raw food. Keep a clear section of counter available and choose pots you can lift to the sink without moving heavy appliances. If the best location is only a narrow sill, begin with two or three containers rather than filling the entire ledge.

Make a short list of flavors you actually cook with, then check whether each plant can grow in the available light and temperature. Avoid buying a mixed “kitchen herb” display before you know its species: similar-looking plants may have different water and light needs. Keep plant labels with botanical names so you can match care instructions to the right plant.

Measure the light and decide whether a window is enough

Indoor food crops need strong light. Illinois Extension says most herbs need at least six hours of direct sun and identifies a south- or west-facing window as a useful location. Observe the proposed spot at breakfast, midday, and late afternoon for a full day; a bright-looking room may receive only a short beam of direct sun. Nearby buildings, trees, roof overhangs, and seasonal changes can reduce the light even at a south-facing window.

Put containers close to the glass without pressing leaves against a cold window or placing them above a radiator. Rotate them a quarter turn every few days if growth leans toward the light. Thin, stretched stems and pale new leaves suggest the location may be too dim. Move the pots gradually to a brighter site or add a grow light rather than responding with more fertilizer.

If using a grow light, use a fixture designed for plants and follow its stated distance and daily schedule. The same Illinois Extension guidance gives 12 to 14 hours under fluorescent lights, with the fixture 6 to 12 inches above the plants; follow the lamp maker’s distance guidance for other fixtures and adjust for the crop. A timer helps keep the daily period consistent. Give plants a dark period, keep cords away from sink splashes, and raise the fixture as leaves grow so they do not touch a warm lamp.

Group pots by water needs and root space

Separate pots are usually the easiest arrangement for a small kitchen because each plant can be watered, turned, moved, or replaced on its own. Use a shared trough only when the plants have compatible light, moisture, and growth habits. A plant that prefers evenly moist mix can struggle beside one that needs a drier interval, even if both look good on the same windowsill.

Choose containers with drainage holes and a saucer or removable tray. Illinois Extension recommends well-drained potting soil and at least a six-inch-diameter pot for indoor herbs; use a larger container when the plant’s mature size or label calls for it. Do not leave the pot in standing water after it drains. A decorative cachepot can conceal a plastic liner, but remove the liner to water and empty water from the outer container before replacing it.

In a shared planter, allow space for mature leaves and air movement. A plant label or seed packet gives the best spacing reference. If you cannot find a reliable spacing recommendation, use individual pots rather than crowding several roots into one narrow box. Keep spreading plants in their own containers so they do not shade smaller neighbors or occupy the whole trough.

Arrange the pots from shortest to tallest only if every plant still receives light. A tall container in front of a smaller one can shade it for most of the day. Put the plants that need the brightest conditions closest to the window and lower or smaller plants where they are not screened. Leave room behind the pots to wipe condensation and clean the sill.

Keep the garden away from heat, grease, and cleaners

Stove heat, oven exhaust, radiator air, and strong temperature changes can dry leaves and potting mix. Leave a buffer between the herb setup and cooktop, toaster oven, or heating vent. Do not place foliage where it can touch a hot pan, burner, or steam plume. A counter location that looks convenient during meal prep may be a poor growing position if it receives frequent grease, salt, or steam.

Keep the pots out of the path of dishwater, household cleaners, and food scraps. Use potable water and clean scissors or snips for harvest. Wash leaves under running water immediately before using them, and do not use a pesticide on edible plants unless its label explicitly allows the crop and indoor setting. Store fertilizers and treatments away from food, utensils, and children.

Provide airflow without directing a strong fan at delicate leaves. Illinois Extension recommends circulation to reduce fungus and disease concerns around indoor food crops. A small fan on a low setting across the room can help in a still kitchen; it should not dry the containers quickly or blow dust from the cooktop onto the leaves. Remove fallen plant material and wipe the tray during routine kitchen cleaning.

Water by checking the mix instead of the calendar

Indoor herbs do not all need the same moisture schedule. Illinois Extension advises watering indoor herbs according to their individual requirements; some should not dry completely while others prefer a drier interval. Insert a finger about half an inch into the mix, then water when the surface layer is dry for plants that prefer moderate moisture; use the plant’s own care requirements when they differ. A tiny pot in a sunny window may dry quickly, while a larger pot on a cool sill can stay damp longer.

Water slowly until a little drains from the holes, then empty the saucer. If water runs straight down the sides, the mix may have become too dry and pulled away from the pot. Add water in two or three small passes, allowing it to soak in between. If the mix remains wet for days, check that holes are open and that the container is not sitting inside a full decorative pot.

Use a watering can with a narrow spout to reach the soil without soaking leaves or the counter. A marked bottle can help you notice changes in water use, but it should not set a fixed dose: plant size, season, room temperature, and pot material all matter. Check before weekends away and ask a helper to use the same moisture check rather than “watering everything.”

Harvest lightly and keep the food area clean

Harvest only what the plant can replace. Use clean scissors, take a small amount from multiple stems rather than stripping one stem bare, and avoid removing most of a young plant’s leafy growth at once. If a plant begins flowering or stems become thin, check light and whether it has been harvested too heavily. The Illinois indoor herb guidance recommends trimming to keep growth compact and cutting stems rather than plucking individual leaves; harvesting technique should still follow the species’ growth pattern.

Wash harvested leaves under clean running water before eating. Do not apply polish, leaf shine, or a household cleaning spray to edible foliage. If a plant has pest damage, isolate the pot from other plants and identify the insect before choosing a treatment. Follow any pesticide label’s crop, indoor-use, and pre-harvest instructions. When you cannot confirm a product is permitted on an edible herb, do not use it on food leaves.

Keep a small pair of scissors in a clean drawer rather than beside fertilizer or potting mix. Wipe spilled media from the counter promptly, and use a tray that can be removed and washed. These steps keep the growing area separate from food preparation without adding complicated equipment.

Adjust the arrangement through the year

Window light changes with season and weather. A location that works in summer may become dim in winter, while a sunny pane can become hot in the afternoon. Recheck the light for several days when seasons change. Move pots a little at a time if they need stronger light, and watch for bleached patches or crispy edges that indicate too abrupt an exposure change.

When traveling, group pots where a helper can reach them, leave care notes by the sink, and ask someone to check mix moisture rather than water on fixed dates. Do not leave a small pot above a heating vent or in direct afternoon sun behind closed glass. If a plant is declining, harvest usable leaves, discard diseased growth, and decide whether replacement is simpler than trying to maintain a weak plant through a dim season.

In a home with a north-facing or shaded kitchen window, a small grow light may be more reliable than moving edible plants to an inconvenient room. Put the light above the plants, keep it away from water, and preserve a clear counter for cooking. If the light cannot meet the needs of the crops you chose, use the kitchen setup for a smaller selection that tolerates the available conditions.

Quick layout plan for a narrow windowsill

  1. Measure the sill and mark the area needed to open the window and clean the glass.
  2. Record direct-sun periods for one day; target a bright location for sun-loving herbs.
  3. Place two or three separate draining pots with trays, leaving a gap to reach each one.
  4. Put the tallest plants to the side that will not shade the smaller pots.
  5. Keep the row away from a stove, vent, sink spray, and stored cleaning products.
  6. Set a small watering can and a washable harvest scissors nearby, but store fertilizer elsewhere.

Example: A renter with a 28-inch east-facing kitchen sill that receives morning sun could begin with two compact pots and one small grow light if growth becomes stretched. The exact combination should be chosen by its species labels and light needs. Do not stack pots in front of each other to fit more varieties; use a shelf only if it is stable and does not obstruct the window.

Kitchen herb garden troubleshooting

SymptomLikely causeFirst step
Thin stems leaning toward glassInsufficient light or too much distance from windowMove gradually closer or add a grow light
Yellow lower leaves and constantly wet mixOverwatering, blocked drainage, or a pot sitting in runoffEmpty the tray and inspect the holes before changing fertilizer
Leaves wilt despite recent wateringDry mix that repels water, root damage, or excess heatCheck moisture below the surface and inspect roots only if the problem persists
Fuzzy growth on the soil surfacePersistent surface moisture, poor airflow, or old plant debrisRemove debris, improve air movement, and allow the mix to dry appropriately
Flavor seems weak or growth is slowLow light, heavy harvesting, or a plant past its useful stageReview light and harvest amounts; replace an exhausted plant if needed

Change one condition at a time and watch new growth. Old damaged leaves may not recover even after care improves, so judge the change by fresh leaves and the moisture pattern rather than expecting a yellow leaf to turn green again.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can herbs grow on a kitchen windowsill?

Yes, when the sill receives enough light for the chosen species and the pots have drainage. Illinois Extension recommends at least six hours of direct sun for most herbs; a grow light may help in a dim window.

Can I grow several herbs in one planter?

You can combine plants with similar light, moisture, and mature-size needs. Separate pots are easier to manage when requirements differ or when one plant spreads quickly.

How far should herbs be from the stove?

Keep containers outside the stove’s heat, steam, grease, and splash zone. There is no single distance for every kitchen; position them where leaves stay clean and temperatures remain stable.

How often should I water a kitchen herb garden?

Check each pot’s mix and species needs rather than watering the whole group on a fixed schedule. Water thoroughly when appropriate, then empty saucers so roots are not left in standing water.

What should I do if indoor herbs become leggy?

Move them gradually to stronger light or add an appropriate grow light. Also check spacing and rotate the pots; extra fertilizer will not correct a light shortage.
