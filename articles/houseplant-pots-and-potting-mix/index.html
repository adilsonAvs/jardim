How to Choose Pots and Potting Mix for Houseplants

By Adilson V. Santos · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated November 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Pot size, drainage, and mix affect how quickly houseplant roots receive air and water. Choose the setup for the plant and room rather than buying the largest pot.

Quick Take

Use a pot with drainage and a suitable indoor mix; size up only when roots or the existing medium justify repotting.

Choose a container that lets you manage drainage

For most houseplants, start with a pot that has open drainage holes and is stable enough for the plant. A decorative cachepot can sit outside a draining nursery pot; remove the inner pot to water and empty runoff before returning it. A layer of gravel at the bottom does not create an outlet for excess water.

Choose dimensions for the root ball and the plant’s expected growth rather than moving a small root system into a much larger volume of wet mix. Pot material also changes drying: porous terracotta loses moisture through its walls, while plastic generally retains it longer. The room, light, and species still matter, so material alone cannot set a watering schedule.

Match the potting mix to the plant

Use a clean mix labeled for indoor containers rather than outdoor garden soil, which can compact in a pot. A foliage plant that prefers even moisture, a succulent, and an orchid do not necessarily need the same medium. Fine, dense mixes generally retain moisture longer than coarse, airy mixes, but species, pot size, and room conditions also matter. Check species-specific care guidance and the product label, including whether fertilizer is already included. Do not add sand, gravel, or amendments by habit; an untested addition can change drainage or air space in ways you did not intend.

When choosing between products, compare the stated plant use and texture with the plant’s needs. If the exact species is unknown, identify it before selecting a specialized blend. For container vegetables, see our separate guide to potting mix for container crops; its recommendations should not automatically be applied to every houseplant.

Use water movement to check whether the setup is working

After watering a plant that is ready for water, observe the surface, drainage holes, and pot weight after it has drained. Water that pools for a long time, a mix that has collapsed, or a root ball that repeatedly dries unevenly can point to a setup problem. Water that runs down a gap at the pot wall may not have moistened the center. Check the root-zone mix rather than assuming any one symptom proves the medium is wrong.

Write down the mix, pot material, and how the container dries under its current conditions. Change one factor at a time so you can tell whether the issue came from the container, medium, light, or plant needs. A cachepot should be emptied after watering; a draining container should not sit in standing runoff.

Decide whether to repot or leave the plant in place

A healthy plant with predictable watering may not need a new pot. Consider repotting when roots crowd the container, the mix has broken down, drainage has become unreliable, or the plant is unstable. A few roots at a drainage hole alone do not settle the question. The University of Maryland Extension guide to pot-bound indoor plants describes a dense root mass and water missing the center of the root ball as useful clues. Inspect gently, and choose the next pot only modestly larger if a move is justified. Follow our step-by-step guide to repotting without damaging roots.

Frequently Asked Questions

What potting mix should I use for houseplants?

Match the mix to the plant’s moisture and drainage needs. Foliage plants, orchids, and succulents do not all use the same medium.

Should I move a small plant into a much bigger pot?

Usually not without a reason. A large volume of unused mix can remain wet too long; choose a modest step up when roots need more space.

Can I put a houseplant nursery pot inside a decorative pot?

Yes, if the inner pot drains and you remove runoff from the outer pot. This keeps excess water from standing around the root ball.
