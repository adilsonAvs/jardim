What to Do When You Find Aphids on Plants

By Adilson V. Santos · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated November 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Aphids gather on tender growth and can curl leaves or leave sticky honeydew. Confirm the insect and assess how much of the plant is affected before intervening.

Quick Take

Isolate indoor plants where practical, rinse small infestations carefully, and use labeled controls only when needed.

Confirm aphids and assess how much of the plant is affected

Inspect tender stems, the undersides and folds of new leaves, and nearby growth for live soft-bodied insects. Sticky honeydew, shed skins, and ants tending a colony support the identification, but curled leaves or ants alone do not prove aphids are present. University of Minnesota Extension’s aphid guide describes their appearance and common signs.

Check several affected shoots and one apparently healthy shoot. Note whether insects are confined to one tip or spread across the plant, whether fresh damage is appearing, and whether lady beetles, lacewings, or other beneficial insects are present. Photograph the same leaves so you can distinguish new activity from old injury.

Choose a low-impact first response

For a small confirmed colony, wipe or remove insects by hand, or prune a heavily affected tip if the plant can tolerate losing it. A firm stream of water can dislodge aphids from sturdy plants; protect delicate leaves and avoid washing insects onto nearby plants. Do not treat every plant automatically. Inspect neighboring plants first and leave beneficial insects undisturbed where possible.

If the infestation is spreading or a plant is too delicate for these steps, identify the plant and pest before considering a product. Use only a product labeled for that plant and aphids, and follow its directions. For edible crops, check the harvest interval. Household dish soap or an unmeasured oil mixture is not a substitute for a labeled treatment.

Rule out other causes of curled new leaves

Heat, herbicide drift, virus, and watering stress can also distort new growth. If you cannot find live aphids, honeydew, or shed skins, do not spray based on leaf shape alone. Compare affected leaves with healthy growth and note whether several plants near a treated lawn or hot window show similar changes. University of Maryland Extension’s vegetable leaf-curl guide explains why curl can have more than one cause.

When symptoms continue without visible insects, ask a local Extension office or qualified plant diagnostician for help. A clear photo of the whole plant, close-ups of both leaf surfaces, the plant name, and recent care changes make the question easier to assess.

Recheck new growth before repeating a treatment

After a rinse or removal, inspect the growing tip and nearby leaf undersides again within the next few days. Look for live aphids and fresh honeydew. Old curled leaves may remain distorted after the insects are gone, so judge progress by new growth and whether the colony returns. If new clusters appear, confirm the identification and assess their spread before choosing another response. If live aphids are absent and new leaves look normal, continue ordinary monitoring rather than repeating a treatment automatically.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if a plant has aphids?

Look for clusters of small soft-bodied insects on new growth, sticky honeydew, or distorted leaves. Check whether live insects remain rather than relying only on old damage.

Can I wash aphids off a plant?

A stream of water can dislodge some aphids if the plant tolerates it. Protect the potting mix and inspect the plant again before repeating.

Should I spray every plant near an aphid infestation?

Do not treat healthy plants automatically. Inspect neighboring plants, identify live pests, and follow a product label if a treatment is necessary.
