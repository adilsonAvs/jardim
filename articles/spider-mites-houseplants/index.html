How to Identify Spider Mites on Houseplants

By Adilson V. Santos · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated November 22, 2026 · 6 min read

Spider mites are tiny and easy to miss. Look for fine stippling, pale leaves, and delicate webbing, then confirm the pest before choosing a treatment.

Quick Take

Inspect leaf undersides with a light and isolate a confirmed infested plant while you plan a labeled, plant-safe response.

What webbing can confirm

Webbing can support a spider-mite diagnosis, especially when it appears with fine stippling, but not every web on a houseplant comes from mites. Check whether new pale marks appear on leaves after isolation and inspect several leaf undersides with a hand lens. If you cannot see the pest, save clear photos and ask an extension service or nursery before applying a treatment.

Check for mites before attributing stippling to them

Leaf stippling can follow other stresses, and ordinary spiders can leave webbing without feeding on a plant. Compare affected and unaffected leaves and look for tiny moving mites near the damaged area with a hand lens. A pale mark or web alone is not confirmation. If the organism is not visible, get a clear close-up of the leaf underside and a whole-plant photo for identification before choosing a product.

A short inspection routine that separates old damage from new

Choose a few leaves at different heights, including one with fresh stippling. Photograph their upper and lower surfaces in similar light, mark the date, and note whether you saw moving mites, webbing, or only old pale marks. Isolate a confirmed infested plant where practical, then recheck those same leaves and nearby plants that touched it. Record only new stippling, live mites, spread of webbing, and new leaf growth; old damage can remain after the pests are gone. If a product is used, record its name and follow its label for safe use and repeat timing. When there are no live mites or fresh signs, continue ordinary observation instead of routine preventive spraying. If new damage continues, revisit the identification and ask a local extension office for help rather than increasing the dose or frequency.

When a local identification is the safer next step

Ask a local Cooperative Extension office or qualified nursery for help when a hand lens and clear photos still do not reveal the organism, when webbing appears without visible mites, or when fresh damage continues despite following a labeled response. Share the plant name, close images of leaf tops and undersides, a whole-plant view, and the short inspection record. Before any treatment, verify that the product label covers spider mites and indoor houseplants and follow ventilation, protective-equipment, and re-entry directions. Do not use a product when the identification or label use is uncertain.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if a houseplant has spider mites?

Look for fine pale stippling, especially on leaf undersides, and check for tiny moving mites or delicate webbing. A hand lens and clear photos help distinguish active pests from old damage.

Should I isolate a plant with suspected spider mites?

Separate it while you inspect nearby plants. Confirm the pest before treating; some webbing and leaf marks have other causes.

Can I use any insect spray for spider mites?

No. Use only a product whose label includes spider mites and indoor houseplants, and follow its ventilation, re-entry, and repeat-use directions.

What does a spider mite look like on a white sheet?

After tapping a leaf over white paper, look for tiny moving dots; magnification helps because color and size vary by species and stage.

How long should I keep a suspected plant isolated?

Keep it separate while you monitor for fresh stippling or moving mites over repeated checks. A single clean inspection is not enough to rule out an active infestation.
