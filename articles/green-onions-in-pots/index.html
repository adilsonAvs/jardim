How to Grow Green Onions in a Pot and Keep Harvesting

By Adilson V. Santos · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated November 22, 2026 · 8 min read

Green onions can grow in a container from seed or from a rooted division, but the two approaches produce different timelines and harvests. Decide whether you want a uniform planting from seed or a small regrowth trial from an intact base, then choose a sunny position and a draining pot.

Quick Take

Use a container with drainage, sow scallion seed about ¼–½ inch deep and thin to about 1 inch apart, or plant only healthy rooted bases; harvest selectively and keep the mix moist, not saturated.

Choose the growing method before choosing the pot

“Green onion” is used for several plants and harvest stages. Scallions, often labeled Allium fistulosum, form edible green and white stalks rather than a mature dry bulb. The University of Minnesota Extension uses “scallions,” “bunching onions,” and “green onions” for this type and explains that they can be started by direct seeding or transplants. If a packet lists a different onion species, follow that packet: bulb onions and perennial bunching onions do not behave identically.

Seed is the predictable way to establish a row or pot. It lets you control spacing and gives several plants at once, but seedlings need time before they reach a useful cutting size. A rooted grocery-store base is a small experiment, not a guaranteed perpetual harvest. It can send up fresh leaves when roots and the growing point remain viable, but the result depends on the condition of the base, light, temperature, and how much stored energy remains. Use one or two bases first rather than filling the container with scraps.

If you want steady kitchen portions, sow a modest first group and reserve a strip or second pot for a later sowing. This spreads harvest dates without relying on one clump to regrow indefinitely. Keep the plant label or seed packet, because names on grocery labels may not identify the exact species.

Set up a container with room and drainage

Choose a clean pot with several unobstructed drainage holes. A container roughly 6–8 inches deep provides a manageable root zone for a small home planting; use a wider pot if you want a longer row. These are practical starting dimensions rather than a species-wide guarantee. The shape should fit your window, balcony, or shelf and remain stable when foliage leans. Avoid a narrow jar filled with water as a long-term substitute for potting mix: it can support short-lived regrowth but offers little room for a sustained crop.

Use fresh potting mix rather than dense garden soil. Fill the container loosely, water it once to settle the mix, and let the excess drain. Do not place gravel in the bottom as a drainage shortcut; drainage depends on open holes and a suitable mix. A saucer can protect a sill, but empty it after watering so the pot is not left in standing water. If the container has no drain holes, use a removable nursery pot inside a decorative cachepot and take the liner out to water.

For seeds, sow about ¼–½ inch deep, following the packet if its instructions differ. The UMN Extension’s garden guidance thins scallions to about one plant per inch after emergence. In a container, use that spacing as a starting point and leave enough room to reach the soil surface without crushing neighboring leaves. If the packet recommends a different spacing for the cultivar, it takes priority.

Give seedlings a bright, even start

All onions need full sun for best growth according to UMN Extension. On a balcony, that usually means a spot with several hours of direct sun and an unobstructed view of the sky. Indoors, a bright south- or west-facing window is a better trial location than a shaded counter. Rotate the pot a quarter turn every few days if shoots lean toward the glass. If the leaves become long, thin, and floppy, improve the light rather than adding more fertilizer.

Keep the seed-starting mix evenly moist while the seeds germinate. A light watering can or spray bottle can prevent newly exposed seeds from washing to one side. Once shoots are established, water at the soil line and allow the extra water to drain. The surface may begin to dry between waterings, but do not let the whole container dry hard. On a hot balcony, check the pot daily; in a cool, cloudy room, moisture may last longer.

Thin only after seedlings are large enough to grasp without pulling their neighbors. Snip extras at the soil line when roots are tangled, or lift and replant a few carefully while they are still small. Do not leave every seedling crowded because the top of the pot looks full: close spacing limits airflow and produces narrow, weak stems.

Plant rooted bases without promising endless regrowth

For a grocery-store base, keep the white root plate attached and choose pieces that are firm, not slimy or dried out. Set the roots into damp potting mix and cover them lightly, leaving the cut top and any green shoot above the surface. Space bases so leaves can spread and you can inspect the soil. Water gently to settle the mix, then place the pot in bright light. If the base has no roots or the basal plate is damaged, it may not establish; sowing seed is more reliable.

Some people start bases in a small glass of water and move them to soil after new roots appear. If you try that, keep only the roots submerged, change the water regularly, and transplant once roots are visible rather than leaving the plant indefinitely in stagnant water. A hydroponic setup has different nutrient and sanitation needs, so a glass of tap water should not be treated as an all-season growing system.

Allow new leaves to lengthen before harvesting. Cutting all the green tissue at once removes the plant’s ability to gather energy and may end the regrowth trial. A rooted base that stalls for several weeks, softens, or develops a foul smell should be discarded instead of repeatedly watered.

Use a harvest pattern that protects new growth

For an established clump, cut outer leaves above the base with clean scissors and leave the center shoots to continue growing. Harvest a few leaves at a time, not every green stem in the pot. If you want whole scallions, pull individual plants once they reach a useful size; UMN Extension recommends harvesting scallions when they are usable rather than waiting for a bulb that this type does not form.

Wash harvested leaves under clean running water and dry them before storage. Keep the scissors clean, especially when cutting leaves that touch damp mix. Remove yellow or damaged outer leaves so they do not sit against the soil. A harvest note with the date and number of stems cut helps you notice whether growth is keeping pace with your use.

Container mix has limited nutrients and frequent watering can move nutrients out through the drain holes. Start with a fresh potting mix and follow a fertilizer label for edible crops if the leaves become pale or growth slows after the initial establishment. Do not increase the dose simply because the stems are thin; insufficient light, crowding, and root stress can produce similar growth.

Make a small planting fit the season

Scallions are cool-season plants and can tolerate cooler conditions than many warm-season vegetables, but a windowsill crop still needs light. In a short-day winter room, expect slower leaf production. A small grow light on a timer can supplement a dim window, but follow the manufacturer’s distance guidance and make sure leaves do not become warm or bleached. In hot weather, afternoon heat against glass can dry a small pot quickly; move it back from the pane if the leaves wilt despite moist mix.

In a container garden, make successive sowings about one to two weeks apart only if you have space and want staggered harvests. The Illinois Extension container-growing guide demonstrates the principle of leaving room for later plantings in a container; adapt it to a narrow green-onion pot rather than crowding all seed into one sowing. Label each section with its sowing date to avoid cutting the newest group too early.

If the pot stays outdoors during a cold spell, protect it from freezing conditions according to your local climate and the variety’s instructions. A pot’s roots experience temperature swings more quickly than ground soil. Indoors, keep the leaves away from heating vents that dry them and avoid placing the crop where cooking grease or cleaning spray can settle on the edible foliage.

Diagnose slow or damaged leaves before changing care

What you seeLikely cause to checkFirst adjustment
Long, pale, leaning leavesLight is too weak or comes from one sideMove the pot to brighter light and rotate it gradually
Soft base or sour smellMix is waterlogged or a base was already damagedRemove the affected piece, clear the drain holes, and let the mix drain
Dry, bent leaf tipsThe mix dried too far, heat is high, or wind dries the pot quicklyCheck moisture daily in those conditions and water thoroughly when needed
Many narrow shoots in one small potSeedlings are crowdedThin to the packet’s spacing; do not add fertilizer first
Little growth after cuttingToo much foliage was harvested or the plant lacks lightLeave the central growth intact and improve light before another harvest

One symptom rarely identifies a single cause. Check the moisture below the surface, smell the base, inspect the drain holes, and compare the pot’s light with the location where it previously grew. Change one condition at a time so you can tell whether the adjustment helped.

Common mistakes with green onions in pots

  • Expecting every grocery-store root end to restart; use seed for a more dependable full planting.
  • Keeping roots submerged in a glass without changing the water or eventually potting them.
  • Sowing too deeply or leaving every seedling crowded after germination.
  • Harvesting the whole clump before leaves have replenished.
  • Watering on a fixed daily schedule instead of checking the mix and drainage.
  • Putting the pot on a dim kitchen counter because the crop is called a kitchen herb; onions need bright light.
  • Applying extra fertilizer to solve thin growth before checking light and spacing.

A simple routine is to check the mix, rotate the pot, inspect the bases, and harvest only outer leaves during the same weekly kitchen reset. If the plant is not replacing leaves as quickly as you use them, reduce harvests or start another pot rather than cutting the remaining foliage harder.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can green onions grow in a pot indoors?

Yes, if the pot has drainage and the plants receive strong light. A sunny window may work; a dim counter often produces thin, slow growth. Supplement light if natural light is limited.

How deep should I plant green onion seeds?

UMN Extension gives about ¼–½ inch for scallion seed. Follow the seed packet if its cultivar instructions differ, and keep the mix evenly moist during germination.

Can I regrow green onions from store-bought roots?

You can try planting firm bases with intact roots, but regrowth is not guaranteed. Light, root condition, and the amount of remaining tissue affect the result; seed is a more predictable way to establish a pot.

How often should I water green onions in a pot?

Check the mix rather than following a fixed calendar. Water when it is beginning to dry, then let excess drain. Small pots in hot sun may need checking every day; cooler indoor pots may dry more slowly.

How do I harvest without killing the plant?

For a continuing clump, snip a few outer leaves above the base and leave central shoots. Pull individual plants only when you want to harvest them whole.
