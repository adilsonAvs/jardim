How to Choose Pet-Safe Plants for Homes with Cats and Dogs

By Adilson V. Santos · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated November 22, 2026 · 8 min read

“Pet-safe” is not a guarantee that a houseplant is safe to chew. Check the exact botanical name against a current veterinary poison-control resource, plan for accidental exposure, and contact a veterinarian promptly if a pet eats a plant or shows symptoms.

Quick Take

Photograph the plant label, verify its scientific name in the ASPCA database for the correct animal, and keep uncertain plants inaccessible until you confirm them.

Identify the plant before judging its risk

Common names are not dependable enough for a toxicity decision. “Rubber plant,” “lily,” or “palm” can refer to more than one species, and a store tag may show only a broad group. Record the scientific name from the label, photograph the plant and tag, and keep the receipt or seller information. If the label is missing, ask the nursery for the botanical name rather than guessing from a photo.

Check the exact name in the ASPCA Toxic and Non-Toxic Plants database and select the animal you have—dogs, cats, or horses. The ASPCA notes that its list is not all-inclusive and tells readers to verify the plant name. A missing result is not proof of safety. Do not extend an entry to an entire genus, a similar-looking cultivar, or a plant with a different common name.

Keep a household inventory in a phone note or spreadsheet with the common name, scientific name, source, and the date you checked it. Save a clear close-up of the leaves, stems, flowers, and label. If you later call a veterinarian or poison-control service, these details can help identify the plant faster than searching from memory.

A careful way to use the ASPCA plant list

  1. Find the botanical name on the plant tag, invoice, or supplier listing.
  2. Search the ASPCA database by scientific name when possible; common names can be ambiguous.
  3. Open the individual entry and confirm it applies to the animal in your home.
  4. Read the listed toxic principles and clinical signs; do not rely on a search snippet or a social post.
  5. Save the entry and date checked, then place the plant according to your pet’s access and behavior.

For example, the ASPCA lists Baby Rubber Plant, Peperomia obtusifolia, as non-toxic to dogs and cats. That statement applies to the named plant in that database entry; it does not certify every Peperomia or every plant sold as “rubber plant.” Even a plant listed as non-toxic is not food, and the ASPCA warns that eating plant material can still cause vomiting or gastrointestinal upset.

If the exact plant is not listed, the name is uncertain, or you find conflicting information, treat it as unverified. Keep it behind a closed door or in a location the animal cannot reach while you ask a veterinarian or ASPCA Poison Control. Do not make a plant-safety decision from an image-matching app alone.

Reduce access instead of relying on “out of reach” alone

Place uncertain plants behind a closed door or inside a secure enclosure if a cat can climb shelves or a dog can reach a low stand. A hanging basket is not automatically inaccessible: leaves can trail down, a cat may climb nearby furniture, and a pot can fall. Keep plant debris, fallen leaves, cuttings, bulbs, and compost out of reach too. Some pets chew dropped leaves even when they ignore the plant.

Choose stable shelves that cannot tip when bumped, and do not position them above a sleeping pet or busy walkway. Keep cords from grow lights, watering tools, fertilizers, and pruning shears secured. Avoid relying on bitter sprays or training alone as a safety barrier; a curious pet may still chew a new plant or a fallen leaf.

If a pet repeatedly eats greenery, ask your veterinarian about the behavior and suitable enrichment. A pot of grass marketed for pets is not a substitute for identifying other plants in the home. Confirm the grass product’s species and keep fertilizers or treated media inaccessible. Even verified non-toxic foliage can cause mechanical irritation or upset when eaten in quantity.

What to do if a pet chews or swallows a plant

Move the pet away from the plant and save the leaf, tag, or a photograph. Note the estimated amount, the time, the animal’s species and weight if known, and any symptoms. Do not induce vomiting, give milk, oil, medication, or a home remedy unless a veterinarian or poison-control professional tells you to. Some products and plants can make a home treatment unsafe.

Call your veterinarian or ASPCA Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 for case-specific instructions, especially when the plant is unknown, the amount is more than a small taste, or symptoms appear. The ASPCA recommends contacting a veterinarian or its poison-control service when plant ingestion is suspected. Do not wait for a symptom to appear if you know the plant may be toxic.

If the pet is having trouble breathing or swallowing, is collapsing, or seems severely ill, go directly to an emergency veterinary hospital and call on the way if possible. Bring the plant information. A website list cannot assess the animal’s condition, amount ingested, or treatment needs.

Recognize signs that need a prompt call

Signs vary by plant and exposure. ASPCA guidance on common houseplants containing insoluble calcium oxalates describes mouth irritation, drooling, retching, nausea, loss of appetite, vomiting, and diarrhea; it also warns that rare severe exposures may cause swelling that affects breathing. Its general plant database likewise says plant material can cause vomiting and gastrointestinal upset. These signs are not a complete checklist for every species.

Call even when the pet appears well if you know it ate a potentially toxic plant. The right response depends on the species, animal, amount, and time since exposure, which a veterinarian or poison-control specialist can assess.

Verified alternatives still need a plant-by-plant check

If you want a houseplant with an ASPCA entry explicitly listing non-toxicity to cats and dogs, the Baby Rubber Plant, Peperomia obtusifolia, is one example I verified in the database. Check that the purchased plant is actually that species, not a different “rubber plant” sold under a similar name. Keep the tag and recheck the listing if the botanical identification changes.

A more pet-compatible home can also mean using non-plant enrichment: rotate toys, add a scratching post or climbing structure, and keep edible greenery separate from decorative plants. If you choose any plant marketed as pet-friendly, repeat the same botanical-name check rather than assuming a retailer’s category is an official toxicity assessment.

Do not place a verified plant where it can fall on a pet or block a path. The “non-toxic” label describes the ASPCA’s toxicity classification for that listed species; it does not eliminate choking hazards, physical injury from a broken pot, or gastrointestinal upset from eating plant material.

Make a household plant-safety plan

  • Keep scientific names and clear photos for every plant.
  • Check the ASPCA listing for each animal in the household.
  • Keep unverified plants behind a closed door until identified.
  • Store bulbs, trimmings, potting mix, fertilizers, and pesticides securely.
  • Save the veterinarian and poison-control contact details in an accessible place.
  • Recheck plant access after moving furniture, adding a pet, or bringing home a new plant.
  • Call promptly after a known or suspected ingestion; bring the label or photo.

Review the inventory whenever you repot or receive a plant as a gift. Keep an unknown cutting in a separate room until you identify it. This small pause prevents a decorative label or familiar nickname from being mistaken for a verified species record.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if a houseplant is safe for my cat or dog?

Find the exact scientific name on the plant tag and check the ASPCA Toxic and Non-Toxic Plants database for the correct animal. If the name is missing, unclear, or absent from the list, treat the plant as unverified and ask a veterinarian or ASPCA Poison Control.

What should I do if my pet ate a houseplant?

Move the pet away, photograph or save the plant label, estimate how much was eaten and when, and contact a veterinarian or ASPCA Poison Control at (888) 426-4435. Do not induce vomiting or give a home remedy unless a professional instructs you.

Which symptoms can follow plant ingestion?

Depending on the plant, signs may include mouth irritation, drooling, retching, nausea, appetite changes, vomiting, or diarrhea. Breathing or swallowing difficulty is an emergency. Contact a veterinary professional for any suspected exposure.

Does “non-toxic” mean a plant is safe to chew?

No. It is a toxicity classification for a specific plant entry, not permission to eat it. ASPCA notes that any plant material can cause gastrointestinal upset, and chewing can create other risks.

Is there a pet-safe houseplant I can choose?

ASPCA lists Baby Rubber Plant, Peperomia obtusifolia, as non-toxic to dogs and cats. Verify that exact scientific name on the plant you buy; do not extend the listing to all plants called rubber plants or all Peperomia.
